Abstract :


The ONCORAD project and the associated radiobiology technology platform are built on the foundations of translational medicine and personalised medicine, and their main objective is the development of targeted radiopharmaceutical products and innovative nuclear medical techniques for imaging diagnosis and improved treatment in cancer. The ONCORAD project is based on the unique competence in the field of radioisotopes and radiopharmaceuticals of the coordinating institute (IFIN-HH), complementing it with the expertise of partner institutions with prolific research activity in the biomedical field (IVB, IC and IBAR), clinics (Fundeni Clinical Institute, Bucharest), as well as in the field of nanotechnologies/nanomedicine (INCD for Isotopic and Molecular Technologies, Cluj-Napoca). This multidisciplinary mobilisation takes the form of four interconnected research projects, with objectives in the field of oncological nuclear medicine. In addition, the ONCORAD project will support the development of the consortium institutions through the recruitment of young researchers and their training in cutting-edge scientific and technological fields, the development of research infrastructure, and the transfer of knowledge, technologies and good practices between the consortium partners and to other stakeholders. The project creates the framework for interdisciplinary collaboration between the partner institutions, thus laying the groundwork for the future participation of the consortium in projects, the diversification of research offers, and technology transfer for clinical applications.

The main scientific objective of Project 1 was to obtain antibodies and single-chain immune Fab fragments against evolutionary surface markers, both for the early stem stages Sca1 (stem cells antigen 1) and for myeloid differentiation (Ly6C), present on the surface of neoplastic mononuclear-type cells in the early stages of myeloid leukaemias. These single-chain fragments will be labelled with positron-emitting isotopes (Zr-89, Cu-64) for use as biomarkers. In vitro detection of leukaemic cells will be performed in cultures using nuclear techniques, and in vivo by micro PET-CT (positron emission tomography coupled with computed tomography, with sub-millimetre resolution). Coupling the single-chain immune fragments with therapeutic radionuclides through complementary techniques may lead to antibodies with a therapeutic role as well, whose efficacy in stopping tumour growth will be tested by in vitro and in vivo techniques. The therapeutic radionuclides to be tested within the project are emitters of high energy transfer radiation (high toxic potential), At-211/Ac-225 and/or Cu-64/67.

The IVB team developed the in vivo model for acute myeloid leukaemia and analysed and phenotypically characterised the tumour cells. Subsequently, the INCD-VB research group tested the new anti-Sca1 and anti-Ly6C antibodies, with a view to selecting the most efficient clones for radioactive labelling.

Results of the IVB team

To generate the acute myeloid leukaemia model, the mouse line STOCK Kmt2atm2(MLLT3)Thr/KsyJ (The Jackson Laboratory, # 009079) was used. The transgene that mimics the chromosomal translocation t(9;11)(p22;q23) is inserted into the locus of the Kmt2a gene. The translocation abolishes the synthesis of the murine MLL protein, which is replaced by the fusion protein MLL-AF9 (MA9). Animals carrying the mutation were selected by PCR genotyping. The mutation causes an epigenetic instability of haematopoietic cells which culminates in complete oncogenic transformation and the formation of the leukaemic stem cell. Thus, both haematopoietic stem cells (HSC) and granulocyte and monocyte precursor cells (GMP) carrying the MA9 mutation are considered pre-leukaemic cells (p-HSC, p-GMP). The onset and evolution of myeloid leukaemia were followed by monitoring the spleen and the general condition of the animals. Figure 1 shows the survival curve of the animals as well as the enlarged size of the spleen (a sign of tumour cell infiltration).

The mean duration of oncogenic transformation is 25 weeks, as shown by the Kaplan-Meier analysis. Figure 2 shows that the MA9 mutation favours myelopoiesis and the growth of granulocyte and monocyte populations (labelled with CD45 and CD11b). The results demonstrate that oncogenic transformation is accompanied by a reduction of the (pre)leukaemic stem population p-HSC (referred to as LSK in figure 2). The second phenomenon associated with the development of leukaemia in MA9 mice is the growth of the (pre)leukaemic granulo-monocytic precursor population p-GMP (preGrMo gate, in figure 2). The results are consistent with a published study according to which myeloid differentiation is necessary for tumour transformation and the onset of acute myeloid leukaemia (Ye M et al. 2015, Cell Stem Cell 17:611-623).

The results presented show that tumour evolution is associated with a reduction of the stem population and, simultaneously, with an increase in granulo-monocytic progenitors. Therefore, the identification of markers specific to these two categories of cells will allow in vivo monitoring of oncogenic transformation. The analysis led to the identification of two suitable markers: Sca-1 and Ly6C. Figure 3 shows the significant results of a set of 4 independent determinations. A reduction of haematopoietic progenitors (cKit+, black rectangle) is observed in MA9 mice as early as the age of 5 weeks, which indicates a reduction of the stem populations (labelled with Sca1). Consistent with this, a reduction of the Sca1-positive population (fig 3, blue rectangle) is observed at 5 weeks, and these cells disappear in mice with acute myeloid leukaemia (MLLAF9, 25 weeks). In contrast, the cKit-positive Ly6C-positive population (figure 3, green rectangle) is maintained in number or increases in frequency as leukaemic development progresses. Therefore, the combined use of these 2 markers indicates tumour progression through the reduction of the fluorescence signals generated by the anti-Sca1 antibodies and the increase of the fluorescence signals generated by the anti-Ly6C antibodies.

After two series of immunisation, the reactivity of the serum towards the haematopoietic cells of the mice was tested by flow cytometry. Immunisation was performed for the Ly6C antigen, and the samples tested came from three rabbits. The same type of cells were labelled with FITC-labelled anti-rabbit antibody and with serum (samples) or without serum (background control), and the FACS diagrams were compared on the basis of the intensity of the fluorescent signal and the percentages of positive populations. In the samples treated with serum, binding of the primary antibodies to the cell surface is shown by the FITC signal. The biological material consisted of mouse cells extracted from the bone marrow of the animals. The results are presented in figure 4. Overall, the data suggest that the post-immunisation serum contains polyclonal antibodies with increased affinity in 66% of cases.
